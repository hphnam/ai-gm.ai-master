\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

Four questions about the flagship forecaster and its detection layer admit a clean
experimental answer, and this chapter reports all four. The first is whether forecasting the
estate's venues together, so that a foundation model can borrow strength across them, beats
forecasting each alone. The second is how much of the exogenous weather path's apparent value
is real once the weather is placed on the footing the model would actually have at serving.
The third is whether the forecaster's uncertainty band is calibrated, and specifically whether
a seven-point confrontation window that read as miscalibration survives being measured with
power. The fourth is whether the published detection-corpus recall and latency figures, measured
against a forecaster that never sees its own injected shift, still hold once the forecaster is
allowed to adapt to it under the production retrain policy. All four are pre-registered
comparisons, all four are adjudicated by the model confidence set, an exact interval, or a paired
bootstrap of Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, and none returns a tuned win: two return a negative
result at full prominence, the third corrects a claim in the opposite direction from the one it
was raised to investigate, and the fourth confirms a headline figure while relocating the real
adaptation effect to an axis the figure was never built to measure. Reporting them together is
deliberate: they share an instrument and a discipline, and each is a case where measuring
properly, rather than trusting an appealing prior or a small sample, is the entire finding.

\section{Cross-series in-context learning does not beat the univariate baseline}
\label{sec:group-icl}

Chronos-2 supports cross-series learning: several related series passed together in one call
condition on one another, so a data-rich venue can inform a data-poor one. The serving path
did not use this, passing every venue under a single shared identifier one call at a time, so
the venues were forecast as isolated univariate series. The hypothesis, recorded before the
run, was that grouping would help most at the sparsest venue, the village hall at Ellel, which
has the most to gain from borrowing a richer series, and little at the Beer Hall, whose own
history is long. Three arms were compared on the same rolling origins: the univariate baseline,
the Beer Hall and Ellel grouped, and those two joined by Two River Taps carrying constructed
post-closure zeros.

The load-bearing precaution is a leakage guard, and it is worth stating why it is not a
convention but a hard check. Under cross-series attention, a boundary error in one series
contaminates the others: if any venue in a grouped context carried a value dated at or after
the forecast origin, the model would be reading the future of the whole panel, and the
resulting inflation would be invisible in the summary statistics. The guard therefore truncates
every series in every grouped context at the origin, the target venue and the non-target venues
alike, and raises rather than warns if any context reaches beyond it. A second property is
enforced rather than assumed: because the pipeline mixes exactly the series that share a
processing batch, the cross-learning group is made to coincide with that batch by pinning its
size to the number of venues and ordering the frame so each origin forms its own isolated group.
Batching several origins into one call is then numerically identical to issuing them separately,
which was verified on the model to a maximum absolute difference of zero, so throughput never
changes a forecast.

The result is that grouping does not help anywhere the evidence can distinguish, and where it
moves the number it moves it the wrong way. On the two hundred and sixty aligned origins the
grouped arms are indistinguishable from or worse than the univariate baseline. At the Beer Hall
the univariate arm has the lowest mean scaled error and grouping is a small but statistically
significant loss, the paired bootstrap interval on the difference excluding zero, though all
three arms sit inside the ninety percent confidence set. At Ellel the fuller group is
directionally the best, in the predicted order, but the improvement is not significant, every
paired interval spanning zero. At Two River Taps the grouped arm is eliminated from the
confidence set outright. The pre-registered adoption rule required a grouped arm both to enter
the ninety percent set and to have the lower mean at the venue concerned; no arm satisfies both
at a venue where the criterion applies, so nothing is adopted and no served model changes. The
capability is real, a grouped forecast differs materially from the univariate one, but on this
estate it is not an improvement.

\section{Perfect weather is no better than a seven-day-ahead forecast of it}
\label{sec:weather}

The Beer Hall's served model conditions on weather, and every accuracy figure it has produced
was measured against weather archived close to the valid time. At serving, a forecast made at
one origin needs weather for the seven days that follow, so the seventh day is a seven-day-ahead
weather forecast with seven-day-ahead skill. Evaluating the model against weather archived one
day out therefore hands it better covariates than it will ever have in production. This is not
target leakage, and the distinction matters enough to name precisely: it is a mismatch in
covariate quality between evaluation and serving, and it biases the exogenous path's apparent
value upward. The study asks how much of that value survives when the weather is placed on the
basis the model would actually have.

Five arms were compared, differing only in the seven-day window of weather handed to the model
and holding the training-context weather and every non-weather covariate fixed: no weather at
all; observed reanalysis at the valid time, an unachievable upper bound of perfect foreknowledge;
the archived-near-valid-time basis that every published figure used; a fixed operational lead;
and the horizon-matched basis in which the value for horizon step $h$ is the forecast issued $h$
days earlier, which is what serving delivers. The lead-matched arms were built from a single
pinned global weather model, because the automatic selection populates the longer leads from a
local high-resolution model that leaves them null, and a basis built on partly-null leads would
measure missingness rather than skill. A gate analogous to the panel-leakage guard of the
previous section enforces that no arm can supply a weather value whose lead is shorter than its
horizon step, verified by value against the pinned store and proven to fire on a planted
short-lead.

The finding is that weather does not distinguishably improve the forecast at any venue on the
basis the model would actually have. At all three venues the no-weather arm shares the ninety
percent confidence set with every weather arm, including the horizon-matched one, so the
exogenous path is, on this estate and this evidence, decoration. The covariate-quality optimism
the study set out to quantify, the archived-near-valid basis flattering the model relative to
the horizon-matched one, is real and grows with lead, but only at Ellel, where the exogenous
entrant is neither served nor better than no weather at all. At the Beer Hall, the one venue
whose served model is the exogenous one, the archived and the horizon-matched bases are
statistically inseparable, and the horizon-matched arm is if anything nominally the better of
the two. The practical consequence is reassuring and slightly deflating at once: the published
exogenous numbers at the served venue are not inflated by covariate quality, because at that
venue weather barely matters, and perfect knowledge of the forecast period's weather is worth no
more than a seven-day-ahead forecast of it.

\section{A seven-point window cannot support a miscalibration claim, and the real problem is the
opposite one}
\label{sec:interval-calibration}

% The xu_conformal_2021, gibbs_adaptive_2021 and zaffran_adaptive_2022 keys cited below are
% expected in the Overleaf root ref.bib (same convention as hansen_model_2011 in
% chapters/methodology.tex): Xu and Xie, "Conformal prediction interval for dynamic
% time-series" (2021); Gibbs and Candes, "Adaptive conformal inference under distribution
% shift" (2021); Zaffran et al., "Adaptive conformal predictions for time series" (2022).
The forecaster's uncertainty band is split conformal, recalibrated on the residuals accumulated
strictly before each block, in the temporally robust spirit of EnbPI \citep{xu_conformal_2021}.
An external assessment of the system read a seven-day confrontation window at the Beer Hall, all
seven points inside the ninety percent band, as evidence of miscalibration: a band that catches
everything, the reasoning went, must be too wide. The claim is checked here on its own terms
before anything else is measured. Under a band that is exactly calibrated at ninety percent, all
seven independent points fall inside with probability $0.90^7 = 0.478$: a coin-flip-scale event,
not a signal. The exact Clopper-Pearson interval on a $7/7$ estimate is $[0.590, 1.000]$, which
contains the nominal $0.90$ and a great deal else. Seven points cannot resolve coverage to better
than a forty-point-wide interval, and the claim does not survive being stated with its own
sample size attached.

Measured where there is power to resolve it, the S2 rolling origins pooled to between twelve
hundred and nineteen hundred interval-observation pairs per venue, the band's real problem is
the opposite of over-coverage. At the Beer Hall the band significantly \emph{under}-covers:
marginal coverage $0.871$, Clopper-Pearson $[0.855, 0.887]$, excluding the nominal $0.90$ and
below nominal at every horizon step within the seven-day cap. This is not an artefact of the
point model measured: the served exogenous forecaster shows the same shortfall as the classical
baseline used for the comparison, so the under-coverage is a property of the band, not of which
point model it wraps. Under-coverage is the unsafe direction for a decision-support band, the
one in which the interval understates its own uncertainty, so this is reported as a live finding
rather than folded into an integration change.

Three calibration methods were compared against the served band on the primary metric, the
Winkler interval score, which is proper and scored in the units of the data: a per-step
recalibration, and two variants of Adaptive Conformal Inference \citep{gibbs_adaptive_2021},
a single online-adapted band and an aggregate over a learning-rate grid
\citep{zaffran_adaptive_2022}. Under a pre-registered rule, a method is adopted only if it both
enters the ninety percent Winkler confidence set and has the lower mean score at the venue
concerned. None of the three methods qualifies for adoption anywhere: the extra machinery of
per-step and adaptive calibration widens the band more than it improves the miss penalty, and
the incumbent is the Winkler-best or tied-best arm at every venue. The one adaptive method that
does restore Beer Hall's coverage to nominal is not a Winkler win, because the width it adds
costs more than the misses it saves, a case where two reasonable scoring rules disagree and the
pre-registered primary metric, not the more flattering one, settles it. A companion contract
prediction, that per-step half-width would grow across the horizon, does not reproduce at this
estate's scale either: the measured per-step half-width is flat, a property of this estate's
residuals rather than of per-step calibration in general.

\section{A recall figure measured against a forecaster that cannot adapt is still the recall
figure}
\label{sec:injection-realism}

The estate's change-point and point-deviation detectors are scored against a 644-injection
synthetic corpus: a known regime shift, spike, stock drawdown, or exogenous-coincident event is
planted in a held-out fold and the detector's recall, precision, and latency at recovering it are
measured. The injection mechanic perturbs the standardised residual directly and back-solves the
raw value that would have produced it, holding the forecaster's expectation and the conformal
band's scale fixed at whatever a walk-forward pass over the \emph{unperturbed} history already
computed. In production, two things the corpus does not exercise both happen: the point forecaster
refits weekly and again on a confirmed change point, and the conformal band recalibrates on rolling
blocks of the residuals it has actually seen. A forecaster that never sees its own injected shift
cannot demonstrate whether it would revise its expectation toward it, so the published recall and
latency figures are, at face value, upper bounds of unstated size. The question this section
answers is how large that bound actually is.

A second, realistic pipeline was built beside the corpus's existing one, left unmodified as the
control arm. For a sampled injection, the realistic pipeline perturbs raw revenue rather than the
residual, applies the change from onset onward to every day the event covers, including days that
later become training data, and re-derives the forecaster's expectation and the conformal band's
scale under the production retrain policy: a refit at a weekly cadence boundary or on a confirmed
change point, whichever comes first, mirroring the live retrain gate exactly. Detection then runs,
unmodified, on the resulting stream. A stratified paired subsample of a hundred and twenty
injections, weighted toward the regime-shift kind and drawn without replacement from an exhaustive
candidate pool, was scored through both pipelines with the same detector, the same venues, and the
same truth records, so any difference is attributable to the forecaster's adaptation and nothing
else. The pre-registered prediction was that the discount would concentrate in the sustained kinds,
where a forecaster has time to adapt within the fold, and be negligible for a single-day spike,
which is caught before a weekly or change-point-triggered refit can absorb it; a large spike
discount would have flagged a bug rather than a property of realism.

The measured discount is exactly zero. Recall and detection latency are statistically
indistinguishable between the control and the realistic pipeline for every event kind: the paired
bootstrap point difference on recall is $0.0$ with a $[0.0, 0.0]$ interval throughout, and the
latency distributions coincide (regime shift, median four days; exogenous-coincident, median three
days; identical in both arms). This refutes the pre-registered prediction in the direction that
matters, since no kind shows a discount at all, not merely the spike kind as expected, and confirms
it in the direction that guards against a bug, since a real defect would have shown up as an
asymmetric discount rather than a uniform null across every kind. The published regime-shift
recall and the reported latencies are not inflated by the forecaster's inability to adapt, on the
axis of whether an event is caught and how quickly it is first caught.

The realism gap the corpus's construction raised is real, but it lands on a different axis than
recall. A change-point-triggered refit fires in three-fifths of the sampled sustained-shift pairs,
and where it fires, the production retrain policy shows measurably fewer alarms after the trigger
than a weekly-cadence-only ablation on the identical perturbed history, in roughly a sixth of the
pairs that reach a trigger. Detecting a shift and refitting to it can make the detector less
sensitive to that same shift's continuation, because the refit absorbs part of the shift into the
new expectation. This never costs the recall figure the corpus reports, because a change-point-
triggered refit cannot fire before the change point it refits on has already been flagged: the
trigger and the covering alarm are the same event, so whichever alarm satisfies the corpus's
coverage test has already happened by the time any refit it caused could act. The adaptation effect
is real and is reported at full prominence, but it belongs to continuation alerting on an
already-caught, still-ongoing event, an axis the published recall figure was never built to
measure, not to the headline figure itself.

\section{A common verdict}
\label{sec:results-common}

The four studies rhyme. In each, a claim or an elaboration that is well motivated, easy to
believe, and in one case already asserted by an external reviewer, either fails to beat a
simpler incumbent, fails to survive being measured with the sample size the claim actually
needs, or turns out to be true on an axis other than the one it was raised to question.
Cross-series learning does not beat the univariate baseline; a richer weather covariate does not
beat no weather at the basis serving delivers; a seven-point miscalibration claim does not survive
an exact interval, while the calibration problem the exercise does find, the opposite of what was
alleged, is real and reported at the same prominence; and a detection corpus accused of measuring
an unrealistically compliant forecaster turns out to measure the same recall and latency a
realistic, adapting one would produce, with the real adaptation effect relocated to a narrower,
previously unmeasured axis instead of erased. None of the four results is a failure of a method
in general; each is a statement about this estate, at this scale, on this evidence, obtained by
the same discipline, a pre-registered comparison, adjudicated by a multiplicity-aware test, an
exact interval, or a paired bootstrap, reported however it comes out. The value of reporting all
four at full prominence, rather than searching for the venue or the metric on which the
elaboration or the claim wins, is that the width of a confidence set, an exact interval, or a
bootstrap difference is itself the finding. Where the data cannot separate the grouped from the
univariate forecaster, the weather-fed from the weather-free one, a well-calibrated band from a
miscalibrated one on seven points, or a forecaster that adapts from one that cannot, the wider set
or the null difference is the answer, and a spuriously precise verdict would say less than the
honest null.
