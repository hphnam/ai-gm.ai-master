# Dissertation Writing: Data Science and AI

## Purpose

Produce a report on the research question, the project undertaken, and what was found, at a standard that visibly sits at Masters level. Four qualities carry the mark: criticality, structure, awareness of readership, and alignment with the marking criteria. Check the marking guidelines before writing anything, and again before submitting.

## 1. The core distinction: knowledge telling versus knowledge transforming

This is the single largest separator between a pass and a distinction. Knowledge telling states what is known. Knowledge transforming tells the reader why and how the stated material bears on the research question.

Knowledge telling:
> Batteries can store excess renewable energy and release it when needed.

Knowledge transforming:
> Although batteries enable short-term balancing of renewable supply (ref), their cost and limited duration make them insufficient for long-term energy security on their own (ref). This suggests a mix of storage technologies is needed to support a fully decarbonised system.

The second version does three things the first does not: it weighs one property against another, it reaches a judgement, and it points forward to a consequence.

### Diagnostic: descriptive moves versus critical moves

When auditing a paragraph, label each sentence. Descriptive moves state what happened, what something is like, the order events occurred, how something works, what method was used, what the options were, and what the components are. Critical moves identify significance, judge strengths and weaknesses, weigh one piece of evidence against another, argue a case from evidence, show why something is relevant or suitable, indicate why an approach will work best, explain why timing or ordering matters, give reasons for selecting each option, rank details by importance, show the relevance of links between findings, and draw conclusions.

A section that is more than roughly half descriptive is not yet at Masters level. Description is not banned; it is scaffolding. Every descriptive stretch needs a critical sentence that earns its place.

Rewrite rule: for any descriptive sentence, ask "so what, for my research question?" and write that answer as the next sentence. If there is no answer, cut the sentence.

## 2. Two levels of editing, in order

Higher order concerns come first. Fixing commas in a paragraph that should be deleted wastes effort.

**Global (higher order concerns).** How the whole text fits together: the argument, the hypotheses, connections between sections, transitions, whether each chapter does a distinct job.

**Local (lower order concerns).** Sentence level: grammar, sentence structure, citation rules, punctuation, spelling, word choice, technical style, expression.

Higher grades are won at the global level. Work global first across the whole draft, then sweep local.

## 3. The project narrative

Every science and technology dissertation carries the same underlying arc. Check the draft tells it in order.

| Stage | Where it lives | What it must do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | Introduction, background, context | Establish the domain and why it matters |
| Problem | Introduction and literature review | Name the gap in knowledge and state the research question |
| Solution | Methodology, implementation, experiments | Resolve the problem via modelling, analysis, design, or experiment |
| Evaluation | Results, discussion, conclusion | Show how well the solution answers the question, compare with existing solutions, state next steps |

Data science aims at knowledge discovery from structured and unstructured data, drawing on computing, modelling, statistics, analytics, and mathematics. AI work overlaps but is more often aimed at delivering a capability or a solution rather than a model. Both processes are sequential and iterative, and the writing normally mirrors that shape. State early which of the two the project is, because it sets reader expectations for what counts as a result.

## 4. Structure and chapter balance

There is no fixed word count per section. Each project is unique, and the split should be agreed with the supervisor. What matters is that the contents page guides the reader through a logical structure and that each chapter does an identifiable job.

Two contrasting real shapes, in approximate page counts:

- Deep learning project: Introduction 4, Related work 4, Text detection 8, Text sequence 8, End-to-end 2, Conclusion 1, References 2, Appendix 10.
- Health service flow project: Introduction 5, Literature review 2, Methods 5, Results 2, Conclusions 1, References 1.

Both are defensible. Neither is a template. What to check: does the weight sit on the chapters carrying the intellectual contribution, or has description of context crowded out evaluation? A one-page conclusion after eighteen pages of implementation is a warning sign, because conclusion and abstract are where the examiner looks for the contribution.

Also check the physical layout: consistent line spacing, margins, and a heading hierarchy that matches the contents page.

## 5. Abstract

The most important section, alongside the discussion. Typical length 300 words.

Requirements:
- Stands alone and is complete in itself.
- Gives a brief but complete overview of the whole report, from aims to conclusions.
- Is not an introduction. Writing an introduction here is the most common error.
- Highly succinct but still flows, with real links between sentences.

Feature set and suggested weighting:

| Feature | Length | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction or background | 1 to 2 sentences | Topic plus the scientific problem or gap; why the work matters |
| Aim | 1 sentence | State the aim plainly now the reader has context |
| Method, design, implementation | 2 to 3 sentences | How the study was done, approach and key facts such as dataset and sample size, kept brief |
| Results | 2 to 4 sentences | Key findings only, with specific numbers and statistics |
| Conclusion | 1 to 2 sentences | Tie results back to the gap; state implications and future direction |

Write roughly one sentence per feature, two or more for the important ones. Reorder and reweight to fit the study rather than following the table mechanically.

Watch for:
- Repetitive framing: "The purpose", "The project", "The implementation" stacked in successive sentences.
- Weak sentence openings that bury the point: "The project will use information gathered" is weaker than "Questionnaires were designed".
- Passive scaffolding phrases such as "is explained" and "will consider" that defer the actual claim.

Put the main point at the start of the sentence.

## 6. Introduction

An introduction must identify the problem being addressed, outline the gap and the motivation for the research, and signpost the rest of the document.

A workable pattern from a strong example: paragraph one moves straight into the issue with no throat clearing, paragraphs two and three set out the overall aims and what the report will do. Application examples belong here to establish motivation, but a list of applications is description; it needs a sentence saying which of them the project actually addresses and why.

Signposting is not optional. Close the introduction by telling the reader what each chapter does.

## 7. Literature review or background

### Purpose

- Provide context and justification for the project.
- Show where the research fits in the existing literature.
- Discuss similar projects, problems, themes, methodologies, and evaluation methods, and critically explore how each is relevant to this project.
- Highlight flaws in previous research.
- Highlight gaps in previous research.

### Structure: the funnel model

Three depths of engagement, narrowing as the chapter progresses.

1. **Long shots.** Background giving context. References are general and are not discussed in detail.
2. **Medium shots.** Work with a bearing on this research. References are summarised, with enough detail to show their impact on the project.
3. **Close ups.** Work with direct relevance to the research question. References are critically examined and discussed.

Chronological, thematic, and methodological organisations are all valid alternatives or complements. Chronological organisation fails when it becomes a sequence of paper summaries; a chronological review still needs to say what each step changed and why it mattered.

Ask what the reader must know in order to understand why the research was undertaken and how it was carried out. Include the key concepts and theories that answer that question, and nothing else.

### Methodological organisation pattern

For a review section covering competing techniques, a repeatable and high-scoring pattern is, for each method:

1. Name and describe the method.
2. Cite examples where it has been used.
3. Give positive and negative points.
4. State applicability, with justification for why it can or cannot be used here.

This converts a list into an argument that ends in the chosen method.

### Integrating sources

Reviewed studies must be integrated into the writer's own text rather than reading as a series of disconnected voices. Patchwork writing is a recognised weakness.

Sandwich rule: begin in your own voice, paraphrase or summarise the source in the middle, and close with commentary on that source's contribution.

Three principles for handling sources (Harvey, 1998):

1. Use sources concisely so that your own thinking is not crowded out by presentation of other people's thinking, or your own voice by quotation. Paraphrase and summary beat quotation.
2. Never leave the reader in doubt as to when you are speaking and when you are drawing on a source. Making the source of any data clear is part of the responsibility of a scientific writer, because it allows verification.
3. Always make clear how each source relates to your argument. Inserting quotations or strings of paraphrase without indicating their use is poor practice. Use sources to support data, illustrate a point, give an opposing view, or evaluate and criticise.

For every citation, answer three questions: why is it here, how does it support the argument or hypothesis, and is it cited correctly.

The hardest skill is critical discussion of others' work, which requires distinguishing your critical voice from the authors you are reading, and indicating your position in relation to the work reviewed.

### Editing failures seen in real submissions

Repeating a phrase twice in the same sentence, introducing a concept then abandoning it for several paragraphs, jumping back to material already covered, and chopped or duplicated opening sentences. These are cheap marks to lose. Read the chapter aloud, or diff it against itself, before submission.

## 8. Methodology

The methods section must contain sufficient detail for a reader to replicate the work and obtain similar results. That is the whole test.

Practical guidance:

- Open with a short introduction to the section that highlights the purpose of the investigation, gives background on materials and data, and restates the aim or problem. This is reader friendly and costs a paragraph.
- Justify every significant decision. Justification is what allows the reader to trust the choices made. Strong dissertations give the reasons for the selected option and the reasons for rejecting alternatives, in full.
- If methods are based on someone else's work, reference them.
- Include ethics approval and ethical considerations where the project required them.

A methods chapter that describes without justifying is knowledge telling in a different costume.

## 9. Figures, graphs, and tables

Strong practice:
- Reference other work where relevant to the figure.
- Integrate the figure into the text, explaining how and why it relates to the objectives, aims, or research questions.
- Write a good figure legend that lets the figure be understood on its own.
- Reference every figure and table in the text before it appears.

Weak practice, and how it reads to an examiner: missing legends, no explanation in the surrounding text, and figures whose function the reader cannot work out. Poor figure handling is commonly read as a sign the author did not understand what they were doing, whether or not that is true. Screenshots of model architectures and unlabelled loss curves are the usual offenders.

Test each figure by asking: if a reader saw only this figure and its legend, would they know what it shows and why it is in the dissertation.

## 10. Discussion and conclusion

Interrogate the draft with the same questions an examiner uses:

- Can two or three key findings be identified, set in the context of the research aims and of previous knowledge?
- Are limitations and implications discussed honestly?
- Did the project answer the objectives and fill the gap and motivation identified in the introduction?
- What lessons transfer beyond this project, and are recommendations for future research made?

The discussion is where the literature returns. Findings that are never compared back to the cited work leave the review chapter orphaned.

## 11. Review checklist

Global pass:
- [ ] Contents page reveals a logical structure, and each chapter does a distinct job.
- [ ] The situation, problem, solution, evaluation arc is visible and in order.
- [ ] The research question stated in the introduction is the one answered in the conclusion.
- [ ] Chapter weighting reflects where the contribution sits.
- [ ] Transitions connect sections rather than restarting at each heading.
- [ ] Critical sentences outnumber descriptive ones in the review, methods justification, and discussion.

Section pass:
- [ ] Abstract stands alone, covers background, aim, method, results with numbers, and conclusion, and is not an introduction.
- [ ] Introduction names the problem, the gap, the motivation, and signposts the chapters.
- [ ] Literature review funnels from general to directly relevant, and every source is commented on.
- [ ] Methods are replicable and every significant choice is justified, including rejected alternatives.
- [ ] Every figure is referenced in the text, explained, and legended.
- [ ] Discussion identifies key findings, limitations, implications, and future work.

Local pass:
- [ ] Grammar, spelling, punctuation, citation format.
- [ ] No repeated phrases within a sentence or paragraph.
- [ ] Main point placed at the start of the sentence.
- [ ] Terminology consistent across chapters.

## 12. When applying this skill

- Diagnose before rewriting. Name whether the problem is global or local, and say which.
- When critiquing a draft, label sentences descriptive or critical and report the ratio rather than asserting the writing "lacks criticality".
- When a section is thin, ask what evidence the writer holds rather than generating plausible filler.
- Quote the writer's own text back when pointing out a fault, so the fix is locatable.
- Do not invent citations, results, or statistics.
